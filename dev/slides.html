  • Loop: FlashAttention-2, Algorithm 1 (Dao 2023), with exp as ex2 and a mask
  • q: bf16[32768, 8, 128]; k, v: bf16[32768, 1, 128] (8:1 GQA)

1Block order


  

    
FA4-style: each query block visits its key blocks from the diagonal leftward, nearest first.

    
flex: each query block visits its partial blocks left to right, then its full blocks left to right; the block mask stores them as two separate lists.

  

  





  
2Why flash attention rescales

  

  

  

  
For one query row with scores sj
  and values vj, attention is a
  weighted average of the values:

  
    out=
    
      ∑jesjvj
      ∑jesj
    
  
  

  

  
To keep exp in range, kernels subtract a reference
  m from every score. With weights
  pj=esj−m,
  they track the denominator
  ℓ=∑pj
  and the numerator
  O=∑pjvj.
  Offline, m is the row max; online, it is the largest
  score so far, and moving it to
  m′ rescales what
  has been summed:

  

    offline
    
      out=
      
        ∑jesj−mvj
        ∑jesj−m
      =Oℓ,
      
      m=maxjsj
    
    online
    
      α=em−m′,
      
      ℓ←αℓ+∑new tilepj,
      
      O←αO+∑new tilepjvj
    
  

  
The example below is one row of nine keys, computed both ways: offline,
  with the whole row in hand (top), and online, one tile of three keys at a
  time, the way a flash-attention kernel does it (bottom). Both halves use the
  same keys, columns and scales. The buttons step the bottom half through the
  kernel's loop (arrow keys work once the diagram is clicked).

  

  
Offline: subtract a reference so exp does not overflow

  
The scores are between 91 and 95. In fp32,
  ex is inf for any
  x>88.72, so every
  es is inf, both sums
  are inf, and out is
  inf / inf = NaN. Multiplying every term of both sums by the same
  factor e−m
  leaves the ratio unchanged, which gives the offline line above. The weights
  are then at most e0=1.
  In the example, m=95 (top half).


  
Online: keys arrive one tile at a time, so m has to move

  
A flash-attention kernel never holds the whole row. It reads the keys one
  tile at a time (128 keys in practice, 3 here) and keeps
  m, ℓ and O
  as running values. It cannot know that 95 is the largest score until it has
  seen tile 2, so m is the largest score so far. When a
  tile brings a larger score, the kernel applies the online line above: it
  multiplies the old ℓ and O by
  α, then adds the new tile.

  
    
    
  1. After tile 1 (scores 91, 93, 92), m=93.
    The weights are
    e−2,1,e−1,
    so ℓ=e−2+1+e−1.
    2. 
    
  2. Tile 2 brings a score of 95, so m becomes 95.
    The tile-1 terms in ℓ and O
    were computed as
    es−93,
    but against the new reference they should be
    es−95=es−93·e−2.
    The kernel kept only the sums, not the individual weights, so it
    multiplies all of ℓ and all of
    O by
    α=e93−95=e−2
    and then adds tile 2. The dashed outlines are the sizes before that
    multiply.
    3. 
    
  3. Tile 3's largest score is 94, below 95, so m
    stays and α=1: nothing to
    rescale.
    4. 
    
  4. After the last tile, the online m is the offline
    95, every online weight equals the offline weight above it, and
    the online ℓ and O end where
    the offline ones do (the guide lines turn green). The online loop is the offline sum done
    in pieces; the multiply by α is what makes the
    pieces agree. Without it, the tile-1 terms would stay at their dashed
    sizes.
    5. 
    
  5. Even if m had stopped short of 95, both sums
    would carry the same extra factor, which cancels in
    O/ℓ. The reference only has to
    keep exp in range; it does not have to be exactly
    the maximum. The next slide uses that freedom.
    6. 
  






  
2Rescale policy

  

  

  

  
FA4-style (lazy): move m only when the tile max exceeds it by > 8 log2 units, else α=1.

  
flex (every tile): move m to the running max on every tile.

  

  

  





  
2Why lazy and every-tile differ in bits

  

  
  
The same sum in different units, not a reordering. In exact arithmetic
  every intermediate of the every-tile kernel (each p,
  ℓ and O) is the lazy one times
  c=emlazy−mnew,
  so O/ℓ agrees. Rounding does not
  commute with scaling, fl(cx)≠c·fl(x),
  unless c is a power of two. Each weight, each addition
  into ℓ and O, the bf16 cast of
  P~, and every tile's extra
  fl(αℓ),
  fl(αO) round
  on grids that do not correspond.

  
If m moved only in whole log2 units,
  α would be a power of two and every rescale
  exact. On a B200, out then differs in 0.1–0.2% of
  rows instead of 87–100%; the rest comes from rounding
  s−m and from
  ex2.approx, which is shift-invariant only while its argument
  keeps its sign.






  
3Scale placement

  

    

    

      
The step. With the softmax scale, the weight from slide 2 is
      e(s−m)/d.
      The GPU has ex2 but no exp, so
      x carries two constants,
      1/d and
      log2e.

      
Partial blocks. A partial block holds some hidden (query, key)
      pairs, whose scores must be −∞ before the max and the
      ex2. On a full block the mask does nothing, so flex compiles
      the loop body twice, with and without it.

    

  

  

    
FA4-stylem in raw units, log2 e / √d folded into one constant ahead of time: one fma in the source, on every block (the mask acts earlier, on raw S)

    
flex, partialsource: × 1/√d, mask, × log2 e, − m; LLVM moves the mask after × log2 e, leaving mul, mul, sub

    
flex, fullno mask, so × log2 e feeds − m and the compiler fuses them into one fma

  

  





  
4Row-sum order

  

    

    

      
The step. Each tile adds its 128 fp32 weights for one query
      row into ℓ, the denominator of
      O/ℓ. The order of those adds,
      and where the old αℓ joins, is set
      by how the tile's rows are spread over threads.

      
FA4-style: αℓ starts accumulator 0.
      flex: one fma adds αℓ at the end.
